The current state of AI.

Three zones describe how organizations work with AI today. Knowing which one yours is in shows which actions make sense.

Zone 01
Basic use
Many organizations are here today
  • Individual tools (ChatGPT, Copilot, Gemini) used by individuals
  • No integration with internal systems
  • No formal use policy
  • Common risk: sensitive data on unassessed platforms
Zone 02
Active integration
Leading organizations worldwide
  • AI connected to workflows and internal systems
  • Active governance, assigned owners
  • Industry-specific models, policies in development
  • Common risk: too little oversight of automated workflows
Zone 03
Agentic AI
Where the world is heading in 2026–2027
  • Autonomous agents carry out multi-step tasks
  • Parallel workflows without human intervention at every step
  • Parallel, integrated execution
  • Common risk: losing sight of permissions and actions

What is AI?

Three concepts under one name.

When someone says "AI" they can mean very different things. Understanding the difference explains why results vary and why the way you ask matters.

① Artificial intelligence Systems that classify, predict or identify patterns. It implies neither creativity nor an understanding of the world.
Spam filters · Recommendations · Fraud detection
② Generative AI Creates new content: text, images, audio, code. It does so by recombining learned patterns.
DALL·E · Suno · Runway
③ Large language model (LLM) Generative AI trained on vast amounts of text. It predicts which words continue a sequence from statistical patterns.
Claude · ChatGPT · Gemini · Copilot
Definition · OECD
"An AI system is a machine-based system that, for explicit or implicit objectives, infers, from the input it receives, how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations, or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments."

How an LLM works.

From text to prediction.

An LLM such as ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude predicts the most likely continuation of a text, from patterns learned from enormous numbers of documents. It does not "think" or "know" the answer.

01
Massive knowledge
Text, websites, books, code and conversations are taken in at scale.
→trillions of tokens in the training corpus
02
Text into tokens
Language is turned into numeric units that the model processes.
Illustrative example:"The cat sleeps"
→ [The][ cat][ sle][ep][s]
→ [9823][1452][7291][3108][615]
03
Semantic understanding
Related concepts are grouped in a shared vector space.
E.g.:cat / feline / animal /
dog / pet → grouped
04
Neural network and weights
Learned patterns are stored in billions of parameters.
→large models have billions of parameters
05
Probabilistic prediction
No single "correct" answer is stored. Each token is a sample from a distribution.
Illustrative example:"cat" → ...
sleeps47%
eats33%
plays20%
06
Generated answer
Token by token, in real time, back to the user.
→"The cat sleeps on the sofa"
⚡
Key point · Non-determinism
The same question can produce different answers. No single "correct" answer is stored. Each token is a sample from a probability distribution. This explains hallucinations, variability and why the prompt's context matters so much.

Section 01 · AI and data governance framework

What is data governance?

Organizations make decisions every day based on information. Data governance is the practice of defining, explicitly, how that information is collected, stored, used and protected.

Its essential components: who is responsible for which information, which rules govern its use, and which processes make sure those rules are followed.

Why AI changes the picture
AI amplifies the governance that already exists, and its gaps too.

Once AI is introduced, data stops being only an input to human decisions and becomes the material the system learns from, reasons with and produces outputs from.

  • Which data may be shared with external AI tools?
  • Who reviews and validates outputs before anyone acts on them?
  • Which decisions may the system make on its own?
  • How do you know whether the system is working correctly?
The three questions governance answers
01 · People
Who is responsible?
Who has authority over each type of data or AI system in the organization.
02 · Rules
Which rules govern its use?
What is allowed, what is restricted, and under which conditions AI tools may be used.
03 · Processes
Which processes ensure compliance?
How the organization checks, audits and updates that the rules are applied in practice.

Section 01 · AI and data governance framework

Principles and first steps.

Five principles for governing AI use, and three concrete steps to start without a full framework.

Five core principles
01
One assigned owner
Every AI system has an owner assigned in writing: one named person, never a team or a committee. Without that person, problems are ignored until they escalate.
02
Active human oversight
AI outputs are inputs to human decisions. Each task gets one of the four oversight levels (full review, selective review, human on the loop or human-initiated), depending on what is at stake.
03
Transparent use
The team should know when and how AI is used in its processes. Undeclared use creates risks that cannot be managed.
04
Protecting sensitive information
Customer data and confidential information should not be shared with external tools without an explicit policy.
05
Regular review
Tools change and risks emerge gradually. Effective governance includes regular reviews.
Where to start

High impact, low complexity.

A policy does not need to be perfect to be useful. These three steps go a long way for little upfront effort.

1
Inventory of current use
Which AI tools does the team use today, even informally?
2
A data protection line
Which information must never leave internal systems?
3
A point of responsibility
Who is the assigned owner for updating and communicating AI use policies?
Rule of thumb A clear two-page starter policy works better than a complete framework nobody reads.

Section 02 · Data governance roadmap

Roadmap: four stages of maturity.

AI governance maturity is built in stages, and each one enables the next. Identify where the organization is today and which concrete actions fit.

Level 01
Initial
Informal use, with no defined processes or owners.
  • Identify which AI tools and data are in use today, even informally.
  • Name an interim owner to track AI use.
  • Document the most obvious risks of current use without a policy.
Starting point
Level 02
Managed
Owners defined, basic policies in place.
  • Assign data owners by domain, with clear authority.
  • Define policies for data use, protection and classification.
  • Put role-based access control on critical tools and systems.
Basic structure
Level 03
Defined
Formal processes running, quality measured with metrics.
  • Turn on data quality metrics, with documented regular review.
  • Build AI governance into formal audits and regulatory frameworks.
  • Keep an up-to-date catalog of the data and AI tools in use.
Formal processes
Level 04
Measured
Full observability, AI integrated with traceability.
  • Automate governance controls and real-time compliance alerts.
  • Enable AI use in regulated decisions, with full traceability.
  • A continuous improvement cycle based on model performance metrics.
Full maturity
The cumulative rule Without defined owners there is no data quality. Without data quality there are no reliable AI models. Each stage is a prerequisite for the next, so levels cannot be skipped.

Section 03 · Organizational blind spots

Eight assumptions that sabotage adoption.

AI adoption often fails because of assumptions nobody questions, more than for technical reasons. Select a number to explore each one.

01/ 8Blind spot
01
The "black box" doesn't matter.
Leaders and teams
Assumption "If the AI produces good results, we don't need to understand how."
Why it's a mistake When nobody can explain how the system reached a conclusion, nobody can tell when it is wrong either.
  • Remedies
  • Require outputs to be verifiable before they are used in important decisions.
  • Document the validation process alongside the result.
  • Treat explainability as a design requirement from the start.
02
AI outputs feel final.
Everyone
Assumption "If the AI says it confidently, it's probably right."
Why it's a mistake Language models produce answers that sound sure whatever their real accuracy. An authoritative tone does not reflect certainty.
  • Remedies
  • Set up cross-checking for any factual claim an AI generates.
  • Tell AI as a synthesis tool apart from AI as a source of facts (which needs validation).
  • Make critical skepticism of unverified outputs normal.
03
AI is objective.
Everyone
Assumption "Unlike people, AI has no biases."
Why it's a mistake AI systems learn from data produced by people and inherit its patterns, including historical, cultural and structural biases.
  • Remedies
  • Review outputs from diverse perspectives before scaling them.
  • Identify which populations may be underrepresented in the training data.
  • Do not use AI in high-impact decisions about people without explicit human review.
04
Implementing AI is a technical problem.
Leaders and teams
Assumption "We need the technology team to sort this out."
Why it's a mistake Adoption fails mainly for organizational reasons: cultural resistance, lack of training, poorly designed processes and unclear success criteria.
  • Remedies
  • Involve the people who will use the AI from the design stage.
  • Define adoption metrics before launching any tool.
  • Treat training as a strategic investment.
05
More data means better results.
Leaders and teams
Assumption "If we give the AI more information, it will give us better results."
Why it's a mistake Data quality matters more than quantity. Disorganized or outdated data produces unreliable results whatever the volume.
  • Remedies
  • Audit the quality of your data before building any AI system.
  • Set minimum quality criteria for the data that feeds your tools.
  • Treat data management as an ongoing process.
06
AI will replace people.
Leaders and teams
Assumption "Adopting AI means cutting staff."
Why it's a mistake This assumption creates legitimate resistance and blocks adoption. In many current cases, AI extends what people can do.
  • Remedies
  • Present AI as a support tool from the start.
  • Design workflows where AI frees time from low-complexity work for higher-value tasks.
  • Keep people's capacity for critical judgment.
07
My data is safe.
Everyone
Assumption "I use well-known AI tools, so the information is protected."
Why it's a mistake Many external tools store conversations outside the organization's control. Informal use can amount to a data breach without anyone knowing.
  • Remedies
  • Check each tool's privacy policy before entering sensitive information.
  • Decide which data must never be shared with external tools.
  • Turn on business or private modes where they are available.
08
The prompt is all that matters.
Everyone
Assumption "If I learn to write good instructions, I've mastered AI."
Why it's a mistake Writing good instructions is useful but shallow. The real leverage is in designing the context: what information the tool receives and how it fits into the workflow.
  • Remedies
  • Invest in designing reusable contexts.
  • Document AI workflows as repeatable processes.
  • Tell tactical use (one instruction at a time) apart from strategic use (context systems).

AI security: a new dimension.

AI introduces risks that did not exist in traditional systems: it works in natural language, produces non-deterministic answers and reaches organizational information across the board, often without explicit planning.

It is also a regulatory and trust dimension: customer data and internal information carry direct implications under the regulations that apply to each organization.

Operational
Natural language removes the technical barriers, so any employee can create exposure without knowing it.
Regulatory
AI outputs can amount to formal decisions under data protection rules in force.
Three controls with the most immediate impact
A data use policy
Define which kinds of data may go into prompts sent to AI tools. It is the control that cuts exposure most with the least operational friction.
01
An approved tools list
Publish and actively communicate which tools are approved. Without this list, shadow AI (unapproved use) is inevitable.
02
Human oversight in critical workflows
Define which outputs need validation before anyone acts on them, especially in compliance and high-impact decisions.

Four questions for assessing any new tool.

Before adopting a new AI tool in a workflow, put it through these four questions. If any has no answer, don't integrate it yet.

01
What data does it need?
If the data is confidential, check the privacy policy first of the tool. Turn on business or private modes if available. If the policy is unclear, assume the data leaves the organization's control.
02
Who validates the output before it is used?
Define the oversight point before building the tool into the workflow. Human validation is the control that stops a one-off error from growing into an operational problem.
03
What happens if the tool fails or gives a wrong output?
A contingency plan must exist before you depend on the tool. External tools have outages, policy changes and errors. If the workflow cannot run without it, you are creating a fragile dependency.
04
What does an error cost?
The bigger the impact of an error, the higher the level of human oversight must be. Calibrate trust to the real consequences of an error. A fluent answer is not a sign that it is correct.
The logic that lasts The selection logic holds even as the tools change: identify the task, assess which tool does it best with the data available, and set the point of human oversight.
Broken or confusing? Before calling something a tool failure, reproduce it yourself. If it fails for you too, it is a tool problem, with steps a developer can follow. If it works, the person did something different or the tool confused them, and that is solved through training, the workflow or the design.

How to build a good prompt.

A prompt is an instruction to the AI. Its quality depends on the context that comes with it. Unless the tool has memory or saved instructions, the model starts every conversation knowing nothing about you, and knows only what you tell it.

Input Context
→
Instruction Prompt
→
Output Result
The 4 dimensions of context
01
Role
Which profile should the model take on? It sets the lens it answers from.
E.g.:"Act as a risk analyst…"
02
Purpose
What is the answer for, and who will use it? The end use changes the content.
E.g.:"For a presentation to the board…"
03
Constraints
What should it not include? Length, tone, format, data to leave out.
E.g.:"200 words at most, no jargon."
04
Output format
How should the result be presented? A table, a list, a paragraph, an email, and so on.
E.g.:"Return a table with 3 columns."
Reusable template
Copy & adapt
Role: Act as [relevant professional profile]. Context: I work in [area / company / sector]. The goal is [purpose]. Task: [A clear, specific instruction]. Constraints: [Format, length, tone, what to leave out]. Expected result: [Table, paragraphs, list, draft, and so on].
You don't need to fill in every field, only the ones that prevent misunderstandings. The more sensitive the task, the more explicit the context should be.

In practice: examples and mistakes.

Seeing the change before and after adding context makes the effect obvious. Then, three common mistakes that cost time and produce poor results.

Weak prompt · click to see With context
"Write an email about the new policy."
"Explain regulatory compliance to me."
"Summarize this meeting."
→
"I'm the head of operations. Write an internal email about the new AI use policy. Clear and direct, not alarmist. 150 words at most. No jargon."
"I'm on the operations team of a regulated organization. Explain regulatory compliance in simple terms, for non-legal teams. One introductory paragraph + three key points."
"Summarize this transcript for the leadership team. Include: decisions, owners and next steps with dates. Three sections with headings. Half a page at most."
1Common mistake
Assuming the model already knows.
The model doesn't know the organization and, unless the tool has memory turned on, doesn't remember earlier conversations. Give it the context from the start.
2Common mistake
Accepting the first answer without iterating.
The first output is a starting point. Ask for changes: "Make it shorter", "Change the tone". Iterating is a normal part of the process.
3Common mistake
Including confidential data unnecessarily.
If the question can be answered without real data, use generic or anonymized information. Keep exposure to a minimum by default.

Responsible use: limits every user should know.

Principle AI amplifies the capability of whoever uses it, and the responsibility stays with the person. The end user answers for what they share, decide or approve under their name.
Don't delegate to an AI when…
The decision directly affects a person: access to services, performance reviews, contract terms or resource allocation.
The data involved is sensitive (customers, case files, financial information) and has not been anonymized.
The output needs formal legal or professional backing: legal opinions, diagnoses, audited financial statements.
The platform has not been assessed or approved by the organization for that kind of task.
There is no way to check the answer against another source before using or sharing it.
Before using an output, ask yourself…
01
Can I check this against a primary source?
If the answer is no, the output should not be used as a definitive reference.
02
Does it make sense in my organization's context?
A technically correct output can be inapplicable or wrong for the specific context.
03
Would I put my name to this information?

Governance

From understanding to action.

Three rules that always apply, whatever the organization's maturity, and three concrete actions for each starting zone.

Always verify ↗
An LLM can look right and be wrong. Before sharing an output, check it against the original source.
Watch what you share
Don't enter personal, confidential or customer data into tools your organization has not assessed.
Report it when something fails
If an AI system produces unexpected or wrong results, document it. Silence keeps systemic problems hidden.

Before changing a task with AI, record its baseline: how it performs today in time, errors and rework. Without it there is no way to tell whether AI improved it.

Zone 01
Basic use
Many organizations today
  • 01Use an organization account for AI tools, never your personal email.
  • 02Find one concrete task where AI could save you 30 minutes this week and try it.
  • 03Check whether your organization has an AI use policy. If it doesn't, ask who is writing it.
Zone 02
Active integration
Leading organizations worldwide
  • 01Document which organizational data goes into your tools and who has access to those outputs.
  • 02Name a human review owner for each active automated workflow.
  • 03Set up a six-monthly review cycle: are the tools in use still suitable and safe?
Zone 03
Agentic AI
Where the world is heading in 2026–2027
  • 01Audit active agents: who authorized them? What permissions do they have? What level of human oversight do they have?
  • 02Put in place monitoring alerts on autonomous multi-step workflows to catch failures in time.
  • 03Update the governance framework to include criteria for permitted autonomy and points of human intervention.

AI glossary.

38 terms Full glossary, with usage notes and links ↗

How AI works

AI agent

Agente de IA

An AI system that pursues a goal by planning steps, using tools and acting on the results.

Context

Contexto

Everything the model reads before it answers: instructions, conversation history, documents and tool results.

Context framing

Enmarcado de contexto

How information is presented to the model (its order, tone and assumptions), which shapes how the request is read.

Context window

Ventana de contexto

The most a model can take in at once, counted in tokens and shared by everything in the context.

Domain

Dominio

The field an AI system works in, which sets its terms, its standards and the cost of an error.

Grounding

Fundamentación

Tying a model's answers to sources that can be checked, instead of to what it learned in training.

Orchestration

Orquestación

Coordinating models, tools and steps so each runs in the right order and a failed step is handled.

RAG · Retrieval-augmented generation

generación aumentada por recuperación

Finding the relevant documents first, then having the model answer from them.

Tokenization

Tokenización

Splitting text into small units, called tokens, before the model processes it. Limits and costs are counted in tokens.

Where it goes wrong

Automation bias

Sesgo de automatización

Trusting an automated output instead of checking it, especially when it reads fluently and confidently.

Bias

Sesgo

Systematic error that unfairly favors or disadvantages some groups, often inherited from the training data.

Black box

Caja negra

A system whose inner workings the people using or overseeing it cannot see or interpret.

Data quality

Calidad de datos

How fit data is for its intended use: accurate, complete, consistent and current enough.

Failure modes

Modos de fallo

The specific, recognizable ways an AI system goes wrong, each with its own cause and fix.

Hallucination

Alucinación

Output that is false or invented, presented as fluently and confidently as accurate output.

Sycophancy

Complacencia

A model telling people what they seem to want to hear instead of what the evidence supports.

Verification

Verificación

Checking an output against something outside the system that produced it, such as the source, the data or a test.

Governance

Acceptable use

Uso aceptable

The rules on which AI tools may be used, for what work, and with which data.

Accountability

Responsabilidad proactiva

The ongoing duty of a named person to answer for an AI system's results and show how they were reached.

AI alignment

Alineación de la IA

Getting an AI system to do what its developers and users intend, not only what was literally specified.

Compliance

Cumplimiento normativo

Showing that AI use meets the laws, regulations, standards and internal policies that apply to it.

Explainability · XAI

Explicabilidad

Making an AI system's output or logic understandable to the people who rely on it.

Ownership

Responsable asignado

One AI system assigned, in writing, to one named person, never to a team or a committee.

Shadow AI

IA en la sombra

AI use the organization has not approved or assessed, such as work pasted into a personal chatbot.

Oversight and control

Audit trail

Rastro de auditoría

A lasting, ordered record of an AI system's inputs, actions and outputs, so its behavior can be reconstructed.

Emergency stop

Parada de emergencia

A tested way to halt an AI system quickly, with named people allowed to trigger it.

Guardrails

Barreras de protección

Controls around a model, such as filters and permission limits, that keep its behavior within set bounds.

HITL · Human in the loop

humano en el bucle

A person reviews, corrects or approves the AI's work at defined points, at one of the oversight levels.

Observability

Observabilidad

Being able to see what an AI system is doing in production, from its logs, traces and measurements.

Oversight levels

Niveles de supervisión

How much human review a task gets: full review, selective review, human on the loop, or human-initiated.

Adoption and value

Adoption

Adopción

Whether people actually use an AI tool in their work. It is measured separately from the value it brings.

Baseline

Línea base

How a task performs before anything changes, recorded so later results have something to be compared with.

Continuous feedback

Retroalimentación continua

A loop that turns evidence from use into decisions and fixes, with an owner for each step.

Decision log

Registro de decisiones

A dated list of each decision about an AI system, with who made it and why.

n of N

n de N

Reporting a result as a count out of a total, such as 3 of 12 people, not a bare percentage.

Pilot

Piloto

A limited, time-boxed trial with a few users, a baseline, and agreed criteria for continuing or stopping.

Process owner

Responsable del proceso

The person responsible for the work an AI tool changes, who decides whether a pilot goes ahead.

Use case

Caso de uso

A specific task an AI system would do for specific people, with a result that can be measured.
